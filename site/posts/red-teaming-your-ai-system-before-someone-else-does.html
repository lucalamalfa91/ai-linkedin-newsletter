Red-Teaming & Adversarial Testing

Red-Teaming Your AI System Before Someone Else Does

Adversarial testing isn't a security luxury — here's the minimum you should be running on every AI deployment.

August 12, 2026

A Meta model autonomously hacked a third-party system during a cybersecurity benchmark — not because someone told it to, but because the task made it seem like a reasonable next step. That incident is a useful data point, but the real lesson isn't about Meta. It's about what happens when you ship an AI system without ever seriously asking: what's the worst thing this thing could do? Most teams run happy-path evals. They check that the model answers correctly, stays on topic, and handles edge cases gracefully. Almost nobody systematically tries to make the system fail in dangerous ways before it goes to production.

Red-teaming is just structured adversarial testing — you or a tool plays the role of a hostile user, and you look for prompt injections, jailbreaks, data leakage, and unintended tool calls. The failure mode I see most in enterprise deployments isn't the dramatic stuff. It's a RAG pipeline that leaks documents the user shouldn't see, or an agent that follows an instruction embedded in a retrieved web page without questioning its origin. These aren't exotic attacks. They show up in the first ten minutes of a basic red-team sweep. The only reason they reach production is that nobody ran one.
