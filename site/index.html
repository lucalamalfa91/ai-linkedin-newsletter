#1 Normaltech ★ 3/10

Why AI hasn't replaced software engineers, and won't

June 14, 2026

↳ WHAT'S NEW Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor published an essay arguing that software engineering — often cited as the profession most exposed to AI disruption — has not seen the job displacement that was widely predicted, and structurally won't. ↳ THE REAL STORY The argument matters because software engineering was supposed to be the canary in the coal mine. If AI was going to hollow out knowledge work, it would show up here first: the output is digital, the feedback loops are fast, and the tooling (Copilot, Cursor, Claude) is mature. That displacement hasn't materialized in any measurable way is a data point worth sitting with. The practical implication for builders: AI is compressing certain task-level work (boilerplate, test generation, documentation) without compressing headcount, because the bottleneck in most engineering orgs isn't typing speed — it's requirements clarity, system design judgment, and coordination. AI handles the easy parts; the hard parts expand to fill the time saved.

Claude's take

The essay's framing — 'won't' replace engineers — is doing a lot of work. 'Won't' is a strong claim that conflates current trajectory with structural impossibility. The more honest read is probably 'hasn't yet, and the timeline is longer than 2023 hype suggested.' Worth watching whether the argument holds as agentic coding systems move from autocomplete to autonomous PR authorship. The gap between task automation and role elimination is real, but it's narrowing — and the essay may age poorly if model capability curves continue at their current pace.

#2 Interconnects ★ 2/10

Welcome to the AGI era of AI governance

June 14, 2026

↳ WHAT'S NEW Interconnects declares we've crossed into what it calls the "AGI era of AI governance" — a threshold moment where the policy and regulatory frameworks being built now will define the rules of the road for systems that may already be operating beyond what those frameworks were designed to handle. ↳ THE REAL STORY The core argument is structural, not technical: governance bodies, legislators, and standards organizations were designed to regulate tools, not agents. The moment AI systems start making consequential decisions autonomously — in hiring, healthcare, finance, infrastructure — the lag between capability and oversight stops being a theoretical problem and becomes a live one. For developers, this means the compliance landscape you're building for today may be obsolete before your product ships. The "one-way door" framing is the key signal: unlike previous tech policy debates, there's no obvious rollback path if the initial governance choices are wrong.

Claude's take

↳ WORTH WATCHING The piece's framing assumes the AGI threshold has already been crossed — or is close enough that governance should act as if it has. That's a significant epistemic bet. If it's wrong, treating current systems as AGI-class in regulatory terms could produce rules that are wildly disproportionate to actual risk and stifle development in jurisdictions that comply while others don't. The honest open question: who decides when the threshold is crossed, and what incentive do they have to call it accurately rather than early or late?

#3 Julia Evans / Wizard Zines

write for 1 person

June 15, 2026

↳ WHAT'S NEW Julia Evans (Wizard Zines) published a short comic on a writing heuristic she uses: instead of writing for a broad audience, she pictures one specific person — often a past version of herself or a close friend — and writes only for them. ↳ THE REAL STORY This is a practical fix for the blank-page problem that hits anyone writing documentation, tutorials, or technical posts. Picturing "me, 3 years ago" gives you an instant spec: what did that person not know yet, what jargon would have confused them, what would they have actually needed to see? It collapses the abstract audience into a concrete one, which makes every sentence easier to evaluate — does this person need this sentence or not?

Claude's take

The catch is that "me, 3 years ago" only works if your past self is representative of your actual readers. For developer tooling docs or AI API guides, the author's past self is often more technical than the median user arriving from a search result. The heuristic is sound; just make sure the one person you pick is the right one, not just the most convenient one to imagine.
