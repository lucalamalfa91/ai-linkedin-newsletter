#1 Andon Labs ★ 7/10

VendingBench

June 4, 2026

↳ WHAT'S NEW VendingBench is a frontier evaluation benchmark from Andon Labs designed to test AI agents on long-horizon, economically-grounded tasks — specifically, running a simulated vending machine business across multiple decision cycles. Tested models span Anthropic's full Claude lineup from Haiku to the unreleased Mythos. ↳ THE REAL STORY Most evals measure single-turn correctness or short reasoning chains. VendingBench forces models to manage inventory, pricing, and cash flow over extended sequences — the kind of compounding decision-making that actually breaks agents in production. If your use case involves autonomous agents running multi-step business logic (procurement workflows, dynamic pricing, resource allocation), a model's score here is more predictive than MMLU or HumanEval. The inclusion of Claude Mythos — a model not yet publicly available — also suggests Andon Labs has benchmark-partner access, meaning results may preview capability jumps before public release.

Claude's take

The vending machine framing is concrete enough to be useful, but narrow enough to question generalization. A model that optimizes simulated restocking decisions may still fail at structurally similar but domain-shifted tasks — medical inventory, ad budget pacing, logistics. The deeper open question: does VendingBench measure genuine long-horizon planning, or does it reward models that have seen enough business-simulation data to pattern-match the scenario? Until the task construction methodology is fully public and adversarially stress-tested, treat top scores as a promising signal, not a deployment guarantee.

#5 Charity Majors (Substack)

AI enthusiasts are in a race against time, AI skeptics are in a race against entropy

June 4, 2026

↳ WHAT'S NEW Charity Majors (Honeycomb CTO) published an essay framing the AI adoption debate as two asymmetric races: enthusiasts racing against time to ship before competitors do, skeptics racing against entropy — the slow degradation of codebases they can no longer fully understand or control. ↳ THE REAL STORY The framing is useful because it drops the usual 'believers vs. luddites' binary. Both sides are trying to ship good software. Enthusiasts accept short-term legibility debt hoping velocity compounds into advantage before the debt comes due. Skeptics are watching AI-assisted codebases accumulate changes nobody fully reviewed, and they're betting that entropy — not a competitor — is what kills most projects. For teams making concrete decisions today about how much AI-generated code to merge without deep review, this is the actual tension on the table.

Claude's take

The honest catch: neither race has a clear finish line, which makes both positions unfalsifiable in the short term. An enthusiast can always say 'we just need more time'; a skeptic can always point to the next incident. What's missing from most of these debates — and from the excerpt available here — is empirical data on where AI-assisted codebases actually break down versus where they hold. Until teams start publishing post-mortems that isolate AI-generated code as a variable, this stays a values argument dressed up as an engineering one.
