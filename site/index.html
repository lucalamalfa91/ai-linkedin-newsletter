#1 Current AI ★ 7/10

Introducing the Gap Map

July 3, 2026

↳ WHAT'S NEW Current AI — a non-profit launched at the Paris AI Action Summit in February 2025 with $400M committed — released the Open Source AI Gap Map (map.currentai.org), a public tool cataloguing where open-source AI infrastructure falls short compared to proprietary alternatives. ↳ THE REAL STORY The Gap Map is essentially a structured audit of open-source AI's weak spots: the capabilities, tooling, or infrastructure layers where closed models still have a meaningful lead. For developers evaluating whether to build on open foundations, it offers a faster way to identify where they'll hit walls — before they hit them in production. If the map is well-maintained, it could also function as a de facto roadmap for where open-source contributors and funders should concentrate effort.

Claude's take

The credibility of a gap analysis depends entirely on who defines the gaps and how they're measured. Current AI is explicitly mission-driven toward open-source AI, which makes the map useful but not neutral — gaps that favor proprietary systems may be framed as solvable, while structural advantages (safety tooling, inference infrastructure, enterprise support) could be underweighted. Worth checking whether the methodology is transparent and whether the map accepts external contributions or corrections, or whether it's effectively a curated advocacy document dressed as an audit.

#2 Josh W. Comeau ★ 2/10

Josh W. Comeau's statement on AI impact on course sales

July 3, 2026

↳ WHAT'S NEW Josh W. Comeau, one of the most respected independent developer educators, reports his third course launch — Whimsical Animations — is tracking at roughly one-third the sales volume of a typical launch. His two existing courses are also down significantly year-over-year. ↳ THE REAL STORY Comeau attributes a significant part of the decline to AI tools absorbing demand that previously flowed to structured learning. Developers who would have paid for a deep-dive course are now iterating with Claude or ChatGPT instead — getting 80% of the answer fast enough that the remaining 20% doesn't feel worth $200. This is a direct revenue signal, not a survey or prediction: a high-quality, niche-specific product from a trusted creator is underperforming in a measurable way. If it's happening to Comeau — whose courses are genuinely differentiated — it's likely happening across the mid-to-premium developer education market.

Claude's take

The catch worth sitting with: Comeau's courses teach visual, judgment-heavy craft — CSS animations, interactive UI — exactly the kind of thing AI still handles poorly. If sales are down even here, the compression of willingness-to-pay for learning resources may be broader and faster than most educators assumed. The open question is whether this is a permanent demand shift or a novelty dip while developers overestimate what AI can actually teach them through prompting alone.

#3 Simon Willison ★ 1/10

Fable's judgement

July 3, 2026

↳ WHAT'S NEW A tip from the Claude Code team (Cat Wu and Thariq Shihipar, via Simon Willison's AIE fireside chat): when working with Claude's Fable model — and to a lesser extent Opus — you get better results by giving the model latitude to decide how to approach a task rather than prescribing the method step by step. ↳ THE REAL STORY This is a prompt engineering shift with real workflow implications. The instinct when building with LLMs is to over-specify: write detailed instructions, constrain the output format, enumerate the steps. The Claude Code team is saying the opposite works better with their top-tier models — treat them more like a capable colleague and less like a script executor. In practice, that means shorter, goal-oriented prompts instead of procedural ones. If you're currently writing long system prompts that micromanage reasoning steps, this is a signal to test stripping them back.

Claude's take

The catch is that 'use your own judgement' is model-specific advice. It may work well with Fable and Opus because those models have enough capacity to fill in the gaps sensibly — but applying the same philosophy to smaller or cheaper models in your stack could produce inconsistent, hard-to-debug behavior. There's also no public benchmark here: this is anecdotal guidance from a fireside chat, not a controlled finding. Worth testing, but worth being precise about which model tier you're actually running before you gut your prompt scaffolding.
